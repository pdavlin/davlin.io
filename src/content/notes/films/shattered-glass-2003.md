The Know Your Enemy podcast guys did an episode on this so I decided to watch it. Great movie, shocked me in a number of ways:

First, Hayden Christensen really was a pretty good actor, and, even though the world has come around on the Star Wars prequels, it's sort of sad that he was so underdirected as Anakin Skywalker that he got pigeonholed out of a better career. He's surrounded by a great cast here too, especially Sarsgaard, who is as good as I've seen him. The whole cast except Christensen has this interesting emotional restraint that I think is really true to life for professional writers. I think it's fun to watch him go from charming, to pleading, to desperate, increasingly theatrical while crying and threatening self harm. There are shades of Sith-tier melodrama here, but it's all a bit more controlled and played by the film as more outlandish than Star Wars' self-seriousness, which I think works a lot better for Christensen and for me!

The fact that Glass frames the plot of the film by talking to a high school class explains who he is: center of attention, charismatic, interested in spectacle and crowd-pleasing but less in fact or professionalism. He works the high school class the same way he does his colleagues in pitch meetings, and the implication (at least to me) at the end that he's talking to that class after being fired, but before his firing is public, provides pretty funny insight into who the filmmakers think Stephen Glass really was.

Obviously, A Lot Has Changed™ in journalism in the past 23 years but the idea of a news publication rejecting and firing a Big Personality news writer feels conceptually impossible now; this sort of exists in my mind as the intersection point between when print news still mattered and when personality-driven news coverage took over. That Stephen Glass, in real life, was close friends with Tucker Carlson is certainly notable. You can draw sort of a direct throughline to people like Carlson or Olivia Nuzzi, ostensible newspeople whose own scandal and fame far outpace the things they're actually covering.

It's also fairly notable that there was a time where publishing fake information online really was a punishable offense, when now it's basically totally normalized [0]. The film feels in context like somewhat of a swan song for print media--The New Republic is laid low by a landmark of internet journalism, able to fact check and discredit Glass using a few resources and a lot of spunk--but watched now it's basically a time capsule of a bygone era of journalism that cared about workmanlike process, details, and society in a way that our content-driven, zero-marginal-cost economy no longer supports.

Plus (to my own shame) I watched this on the video platform owned by the guy who recently fired ⅓ of the Washington Post newsroom. Screw that guy!
