An unbelievable and deeply uncomfortable portrait of grief. Lily Gladstone's howls--emptying her soul--pierced me every time her character learned of a family member's death. There's no salvation for Mollie. No justice, no reprieve, not even a beat in this film where she can process the scope and volume of loss perpetuated against her by the man she chose to spend her life with. It's as unrelenting and punishing as it is heartbreaking.

I think it's interesting that Scorsese ends this film the way he does. I understand it a little bit as an indictment of "true crime culture" and its flattening (and, often, whitening) of real pain in people's lives. In the distance, in the aggregate, as we no longer see DiCaprio or De Niro on screen, their inevitable release feels like a foregone conclusion. It's impossible to feel anything but pure revulsion toward their characters. What must their reunions with family have been like after serving time in jail? How could a community so destroyed by their actions--literally, in the case of an exploding house--allow them back in?

It's such a departure for Scorsese, even though at its core it's what he does best--some men, obsessed with material wealth and power over others--but it forces so much more uncomfortable reckoning with history as recent as a hundred years ago. I'll be processing it for a while.
