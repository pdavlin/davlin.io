I have been to a movie theater seven times in the past twelve months, and three of those trips were to see Dune Part 2 in IMAX. I should really see more movies.

I grew up a Star Wars kid and this is probably the last time I’ll write about this movie (everyone in my life is tired of hearing me gush about it), so I have a bunch of loose thoughts about the comparison that I fully acknowledge are probably hackish (comparing them at all, deliberately, in writing, is probably hackish. I’m sure this has been done a million times. I’m so sorry). Those thoughts are mostly presented as unconnected rambling here.

I find such irony in how much Villinueve’s Dune movies have to do to distinguish themselves from Lucas’ derivative work. I have such a clear picture of the visual language of the Star Wars films—Death Stars, TIE Fighters, and X-Wings appear vividly in my mind’s eye—and yet I’d never considered the ornithopters or Harkonnen airships or the Emperor’s disco ball spaceship. How delightful it is, even a third time, to see their mechanics in action. Really just the best CGI world building I’ve seen in a science fiction film in a while.

Everything about my approach to this third viewing felt informed by learning how Herbert felt Lucas owed him more for Star Wars’ success. Paul feels like the natural inverse of Luke, even though their story is the same. Really, Star Wars has been telling this tale—of bloodlines, destiny, mystical purpose—for fifty years, and yet it feels like Dune presents an inescapably different conclusion. Paul is burdened by the depth of his power, weight of his responsibility, and the inevitability of his own success. There’s no medal ceremony and grand fanfare at the end of the film. Paul is doomed to repeat the cycle of power he’s just broken, loosing a power he can’t control. There is no new hope after destroying the Death Star, only the consequences of a conflict that has repeated, over and over, for ten thousand years of history in Dune’s lore. 

The end of Star Wars feels like a triumph because the odds are so long, and the Empire is so overwhelming. The conflict in Dune carries this weight of inevitability throughout. We’re introduced to more Harkonnens and an Emperor who are already too late to stop the Fremen; the third act nearly dismisses them altogether. We’re left to wonder whether they were ever really a threat at all. Maybe the real question is whether the ends justify the means—how many deaths make Paul’s ascent worthwhile? Is it worth it to be hated by the Great Houses? By the Bene Gesserit? By Chani? What happens when you get what you always wanted, what you’ve *earned*, by nature of being born who you are, with the rewards and punishments that follow? It feels like I’m supposed to wonder whether getting what you want sometimes requires losing yourself along the way, and that maybe you just become worse for it. Paul has every opportunity to live in the desert, with Chani, to reject the empire and build a better world with the Fremen on Dune. 

Maybe we’re supposed to just accept some things, take stock of where we are, and who we love, and make peace with that. Maybe we’re not supposed to sit on the throne at all.
