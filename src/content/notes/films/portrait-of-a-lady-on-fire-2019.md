_This review may contain spoilers._

Seems odd to me that an orchestra would start Vivaldi's Four Seasons from The Storm movement of Summer (or, six movements into the piece) but maybe they knew it was important to the story???

This may be the most beautiful film I've ever watched. The choice not to score the film makes the two moments with diegetic music incredibly powerful. But the sound design in general lends the movie this intense, larger-than-life feeling that deeply enhances the story. The rhythms of waves crashing, paintbrushes dragged against canvas, and creaks of floors all propel Portrait of a Lady on Fire, frantically and heaving, to its conclusion. As heartbreaking as it is intense.

The story of Eurydice and Orpheus lends a gentle, English teacher-ish vibe to the story that does enhance it for the Moron Viewer (me). The allegory to Marianne and Héloïse is plain, and yet is presented ambiguously enough that the viewer still needs to interpret its meaning. The question at hand, for me, is not which character is Orpheus or Eurydice but rather whether they might represent two different outcomes of the allegory. Marianna enters the story as the experienced one; she's loved and lost, she's heard orchestral music and been to Milan. Her journey to the island is, allegorically, her descent to Hades, armed with a canvas instead of a lyre and surrounded by women from a convent rather than shades. She is, naturally, doomed to look back, to see Héloïse, a shade in her wedding gown, and lose her forever, frozen in that moment, and sentenced to a life of producing art that can only ever recreate that moment. From Marianne's perspective, as the narrator, Héloïse doesn't look back; she has a life, a husband, a child. She sits in the box seats at the orchestra; she has more portraits painted. And yet, there are signs that she looks back to Marianne constantly, that her connection to art and to life is defined by her love. That's right: they're both Orpheus! One making the poet's choice and another, the lover's choice, each in a different sort of suspended animation in that permanent moment of recognition.
